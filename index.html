About

My research sits at the intersection of sociology, migration studies, and public policy. I am particularly interested in how institutions shape inequality over the life course, and how abrupt policy changes can create measurable differences in migrants’ access to work, welfare, and long-term social integration.

Before Oxford, I worked across academic and policy settings including the OECD, EFTA, the University of Iceland, and the Icelandic public sector. Across these roles, I developed a strong interest in combining rigorous empirical methods with questions that matter for contemporary migration governance.

Research

Legal Certainty and Early Integration: Causal Evidence from a Migration Shock in Iceland

This doctoral project examines how the timing and certainty of legal status acquisition affect migrants’ labour market and socio-economic integration. It exploits a short-lived change in asylum policy in Iceland that created sharp differences in legal certainty across closely spaced arrival cohorts.

Using population-wide administrative data and quasi-experimental methods, the project estimates the causal effects of early institutional conditions on employment, welfare dependence, and longer-term integration trajectories.

Intermarriage and Integration in Iceland (with Kolbeinn Stefánsson)

I am currently working on a co-authored project on intermarriage in Iceland with Professor Kolbeinn Stefánsson (University of Iceland; DPhil Sociology, Oxford). Using population-wide register data with unique capacity to identify long-term cohabitation, the project represents the first systematic study of intermarriage in Iceland.

In a context of low formal marriage rates, this data structure allows a more accurate capture of mixed-union formation than marriage records alone. Methodologically, we employ event-study and fixed-effects designs to distinguish selection into intermarriage from causal “intermarriage premiums” in socioeconomic outcomes.

Preliminary results point strongly toward selection rather than post-union gains, suggesting that observed advantages largely reflect pre-union characteristics. The project contributes to debates on integration, family formation, and inequality in high-cohabitation welfare states and is currently being prepared for journal submission.
